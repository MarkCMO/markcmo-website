Executive Leadership for Companies Between Hires

The CMO just left. The COO is moving on. You have a business that needs senior executive leadership today, and a hiring process that will take 6 months minimum. The gap between those two realities is where companies lose momentum, lose team members, and lose customers.

The True Cost of the Empty Executive Chair

When a senior executive leaves, most companies focus on the hiring problem. They engage a recruiter, post the role, begin evaluating candidates. What they often underestimate is the cost of the gap itself - the period between when the executive departs and when a replacement is in place and productive. In my experience, that gap is the most dangerous period in the life of a company's marketing or operations function, and the damage it causes is almost always invisible until it has already become significant.

The direct costs are easy to see. Executive recruiting fees typically run 25-30% of first-year compensation. Onboarding a new C-suite executive takes 3-6 months before they are genuinely productive. During that time, the department they lead is operating without strategic direction. But the indirect costs are far more expensive. Team members who lack senior leadership become uncertain about priorities and direction. High performers, who always have options, begin testing the market. Campaigns pause, projects stall, and decisions that needed to be made last month are still pending. Vendors and agency partners sense the leadership vacuum and become less invested in the account.

The average executive search for a CMO or COO takes 6.3 months from the moment the search is launched. During those 6.3 months, the business is running without the strategic leadership function that role provided. If the departing executive held institutional knowledge about key relationships, vendor contracts, campaign strategies, or operational decisions that were never fully documented, that knowledge walks out the door with them. I have seen companies lose 20-30% of their marketing pipeline effectiveness during a CMO transition simply because the institutional knowledge of what was working and why was not captured and transferred.

There is also the team dimension. Marketing and operations teams are often built around the leadership style of the executive who built them. When that executive leaves, the team frequently experiences a period of genuine anxiety - not about their own job security necessarily, but about direction, priorities, and what the future looks like. That anxiety, if not actively managed by a capable senior leader, translates into reduced output, increased politics, and the beginning of a talent drain that can be very difficult to reverse.

Day One: What I Do Immediately When I Step In

Speed matters in a transition engagement. Every day without senior leadership is a day the team is operating without direction, a day campaigns are running without strategic oversight, and a day that the institutional knowledge gap is widening. When I step into a between-hires engagement, I follow a structured Day One protocol designed to establish presence, build trust, and begin protecting value immediately.

The first thing I do is meet individually with every direct report of the departing executive. Not as a group - individually, privately, and with genuine curiosity rather than an agenda. These conversations serve two purposes. First, they give me a rapid inventory of what each person knows, what they are worried about, what projects are in flight, and what decisions are pending. Second, they signal to the team that the new senior leadership is paying attention to them as individuals, not just to the function they perform. In a transition period, that signal matters more than most executives realize.

Within the first 48 hours, I review every active project, campaign, and vendor relationship within the function. I am looking for three things: what is on fire and needs immediate attention, what is in good shape and should continue without interference, and what is unclear or undocumented and needs to be understood before any decisions are made. This triage prevents the most common transition mistake, which is making changes before you understand the current state well enough to know what you are changing from.

In the first week, I hold a team meeting that is deliberately different from what many teams expect. I do not arrive with a vision statement or a new strategic direction. I arrive with acknowledgment of the transition, honest communication about the situation, a clear statement of my immediate priorities, and an invitation for the team to share what they need from senior leadership to do their best work right now. That conversation, done well, can meaningfully reduce the anxiety and uncertainty that is the biggest productivity killer in a transition period.

Stabilizing the Team During Executive Transition

The talent risk in an executive transition is real and it is underestimated by most leadership teams. The people most likely to leave during a leadership gap are exactly the people you can least afford to lose: the high performers who have strong relationships with the departing executive, who have options in the market, and who are paying attention to signals about the company's direction and culture. Keeping these people requires more than reassurance. It requires demonstrable senior leadership that makes them feel confident about the future of the team and the function.

I approach team stabilization with the understanding that what people need during uncertainty is not cheerleading - it is clarity. Clarity about direction, clarity about decisions, clarity about what success looks like right now. I establish a regular cadence of communication with the team that is honest about what I know, honest about what I don't know yet, and explicit about the process I am using to make decisions in the interim. That transparency, consistently maintained, is the most powerful retention tool available during a transition.

I also pay close attention to the informal leaders in the team - the people who don't have formal authority but who others look to for signals about how to feel about the situation. These people can be powerful allies or powerful sources of anxiety, depending on how they are engaged. I identify them quickly and invest time in understanding their perspective and concerns, because they will shape the team's emotional response to the transition more than any formal communication I send.

For team members who are genuinely at flight risk, I work with the CEO or relevant board members to establish a clear value proposition for staying: a compelling vision for the function, a realistic picture of the hiring timeline, and where appropriate, retention mechanisms that demonstrate the company's commitment to the team's future. I have retained teams through transitions that looked very difficult simply by communicating honestly and following through on every commitment made.

Protecting Momentum: Keeping Campaigns, Systems, and Operations Running

One of the most damaging things that happens during an executive gap is momentum loss. A campaign that was producing pipeline quietly degrades because nobody with strategic authority is making the optimization decisions it needs. A vendor relationship that was producing value goes into maintenance mode because nobody is engaging at the right level. An operational initiative that was mid-implementation stalls because the decision-maker who was driving it has left. By the time the new executive arrives, what should have been a running start becomes a cleanup job.

Protecting momentum in a transition engagement requires both operational capability and strategic judgment. On the operational side, I take ownership of all active campaigns, initiatives, and vendor relationships from day one. I meet with every key vendor and agency partner within the first two weeks to signal continued engagement and strategic direction. I review every active campaign and make the optimization decisions that need to be made to keep performance stable while the search is in progress.

On the strategic side, I work with the CEO and leadership team to ensure that the significant decisions that need to be made during the transition period are made with appropriate rigor, not deferred indefinitely or made impulsively. A between-hires period is not the right time to make transformational strategic changes, but it is also not the right time to put everything on hold. I help distinguish between decisions that should wait for the permanent hire and decisions that need to be made now to protect the business.

I also use the transition period to document the institutional knowledge that is at risk of being lost. What were the strategic logic and assumptions behind key decisions? What does the team know about why certain campaigns or initiatives work? What are the unwritten rules and norms that make the function operate smoothly? This documentation serves two purposes: it preserves knowledge that would otherwise walk out the door, and it gives the incoming permanent executive a much richer starting point than the typical "here's the shared drive, good luck" onboarding that most senior hires receive.

The Transition Playbook: Handoff to the Permanent Hire

My goal in a between-hires engagement is to make the incoming permanent hire as successful as possible from day one. This is a different objective than most interim executives pursue, and it shapes everything I do during the engagement. Instead of protecting my relevance or building dependence on my continued involvement, I am actively building the conditions for a successful handoff.

The transition playbook I develop during the engagement includes several components. The first is a complete state-of-the-function document: where the team stands on every active initiative, the current performance of every channel and campaign, the status of every vendor relationship, and the key decisions that have been made and why. This document is written for the permanent hire, not for me - it assumes they are coming in cold and need to understand the context for everything they are inheriting.

The second component is a strategic brief: what I believe the function's biggest opportunities and challenges are at the moment of handoff, what strategic options are available, and what early decisions I would recommend the incoming executive consider. This is explicitly framed as perspective, not prescription - the permanent executive will develop their own view, and my job is to give them a head start on the thinking, not to constrain their approach.

The third component is relationship documentation. Who are the key relationships inside and outside the organization that the incoming executive needs to build quickly? What are the nuances of those relationships? What history exists between those relationships and the function that the permanent hire needs to understand? Senior executive relationships are one of the most underestimated sources of value in a C-suite role, and transferring relationship context thoughtfully can meaningfully accelerate a new hire's integration.

I also offer to participate in the final-stage interviewing for the permanent role, if the CEO finds that useful. Not to make the hiring decision, but to provide a technical and cultural perspective that is grounded in the actual state of the function. I have found that this participation, when it happens, consistently results in better hiring decisions because the candidates can be evaluated against the real current needs of the function rather than a job description that may have been written 6 months earlier.

How to Decide: Fractional Long-Term vs. Hiring Full-Time

One of the conversations I have with almost every company that engages me for a between-hires situation is whether hiring a permanent full-time executive is actually the right answer. Sometimes it is clearly the right answer. Sometimes it is less clear than the company thinks. The decision deserves more rigorous analysis than most companies give it, because the cost of a wrong hire at the C-suite level - in time, money, culture, and momentum - is enormous.

The case for a full-time hire is strongest when the function requires a significant volume of senior decision-making that genuinely demands a dedicated leader's attention, when the company is at a size and stage where the cost of a full-time executive is clearly justified by the revenue or operational responsibility involved, and when the cultural importance of having someone fully committed to the company's mission and values in a senior leadership role outweighs the efficiency arguments for a fractional arrangement.

The case for a long-term fractional arrangement is strongest when the function needs strategic leadership but not full-time executive capacity, when the company is at a stage where a $300,000-$450,000 annual executive salary would be better deployed elsewhere, when the nature of the work is episodic rather than constant, or when the company wants access to senior leadership across multiple disciplines - marketing and operations, for example - that would require two full-time hires but can be served by one experienced fractional executive.

I am direct with the companies I work with about this analysis. My goal is not to perpetuate my engagement beyond what serves the company's actual interests. If the right answer is a full-time hire, I will say so clearly and help design the search and selection process. If the right answer is a long-term fractional relationship, I will make that case with specifics. The companies that benefit most from this kind of honest counsel are the ones that enter the conversation willing to follow the analysis wherever it leads, rather than assuming the answer before they have examined the question.

Frequently Asked Questions

How quickly can you actually start?
In most cases, I can begin an engagement within 5-7 business days of a signed agreement. In genuine emergencies - an executive who has departed unexpectedly and left a critical function leaderless - I have compressed that to 48 hours. The onboarding process I use is designed for rapid immersion, so the lag between start date and productive senior leadership is much shorter than what a permanent hire would require. A permanent C-suite hire typically needs 60-90 days before they are genuinely productive. I am productive in the first week.
Does working with a fractional executive signal to the team that the company is in trouble?
It signals the opposite when handled correctly. Bringing in experienced senior leadership immediately after an executive departure signals that the company takes its leadership seriously, moves decisively, and is not willing to let the function drift while it recruits. The framing matters enormously. I work with CEOs on exactly how to communicate the transition to the team in a way that builds confidence rather than creating anxiety. A well-communicated fractional arrangement is received very differently from a vague "we are figuring out the situation" message that leaves the team to fill the uncertainty gap with their worst fears.
What happens if you find a permanent hire faster than expected?
I am structured for this. Between-hires engagements are designed to terminate cleanly when the permanent hire is in place, with no penalty and no incentive for me to slow the search. I actively participate in the transition to the permanent hire and want that transition to happen as smoothly and as quickly as the right candidate can be found. The engagement agreement is month-to-month after an initial commitment period, which protects the company from being locked in if the search moves faster than anticipated.
Can you help with the search for the permanent hire while serving in the interim role?
Yes, and this is one of the underappreciated benefits of a fractional engagement during a search. Because I am embedded in the function, I have a clearer picture of what the permanent hire actually needs to be able to do, what the team needs from their next leader, and what the right candidate profile looks like in practice rather than on paper. I can brief the recruiting firm on a more accurate job description, participate in late-stage interviews to evaluate candidates against real functional needs, and help design the offer structure and onboarding plan for the person who is ultimately hired.
What if the company decides during the engagement that it wants to keep me long-term rather than hire a permanent executive?
This happens more often than companies expect. About 30% of between-hires engagements I start convert to longer-term fractional relationships because the company realizes partway through the search that the fractional model is actually a better fit for their stage and needs than a full-time hire. I am open to this conversation if and when it arises, and I approach it the same way I approach the initial decision - with honest analysis of what actually serves the company's interests, not with an agenda to perpetuate the engagement.
