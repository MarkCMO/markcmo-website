Brevard Lagoon Initiative / The Port Canaveral Plant
The circular desalination pitch

Water, salt, and a cleaner lagoon

A desalination plant at Port Canaveral that makes drinking water for Brevard, harvests salt from the brine for a partner like Morton, and pulls nitrogen and phosphorus out of the Indian River Lagoon - turning the usual brine liability into three products from one facility.

The pitch in one paragraph

Turn the brine problem into product lines

Every desalination plant faces the same problem: reverse osmosis makes fresh water by concentrating everything it removes - salt, minerals, nitrogen, phosphorus - into a briny reject stream that normally has to be injected deep underground at high cost. This plan flips that. Instead of paying to bury the brine, we mine it: the salt becomes a product for a salt company, the concentrated nitrogen and phosphorus become a lagoon-restoration benefit (and a harvestable crop), and the fresh water supplies a county that is running short. One intake, three revenue lines, and far less to dispose of.

The honest frame

Drinking water is the proven, bankable core - Brevard genuinely needs new supply. Salt harvesting is real and has a working precedent, but on bulk salt alone it offsets disposal cost rather than printing profit. Nutrient capture is a genuine restoration co-benefit, not a cash cow. Pitch this as a water utility that refuses to waste its brine - credible, fundable, and honest - not as a money machine.

01

Product one - drinking water

The proven core. Real, rising demand that the aquifer can't keep meeting.

Brevard's largest system (City of Cocoa) already runs about 20.3 MGD average and 27 MGD peak, serving ~298,000 people, and the county's own water-supply plan projects demand up ~34% by 2035 - explicitly stating it must find new freshwater sources as the aquifer strains. Seawater and brackish reverse osmosis is fully proven (Tampa Bay runs the largest US seawater plant), so the water side is not a science question - it's an engineering and financing one. A 10 to 25 MGD plant sells every gallon it makes at roughly $4 to $6 per 1,000 gallons, which is what makes the whole facility bankable through utility revenue bonds.

Why this pays for the rest

Water sales are the anchor tenant. They service the debt and cover operations, which means the salt and nutrient systems don't have to be profit centers - they just have to beat the cost of the deep-well injection they replace. See the financial model for the utility pro-forma.

WETYR Water

Free water, paid by the brands on the bottle

The plant's public face is WETYR Water - free, ad-supported bottled water handed out across the Space Coast. Local businesses and national brands sponsor the bottle; the water is free to residents. Every bottle is a mobile billboard whose ad revenue funds the lagoon cleanup, and every label says the water was made here and helps clean the Indian River Lagoon.

How the model works

Proven format (FreeWater and others): ads printed on aluminum bottles and paper cartons - no plastic. An advertiser pays roughly $1 per bottle, distribution partners keep a slice, and a fixed share of every bottle is donated - here, to the lagoon. The consumer pays nothing.

The brand story

"Space Coast businesses put their name on water that cleans the Indian River Lagoon." It ties the plant, the cleanup, and the community into one visible symbol - a bottle people hold, refill, and associate with saving the lagoon. That is a marketing engine, not just a beverage.

The honest read

On raw ad efficiency, a physical bottle is expensive - roughly a $1,000 cost per thousand impressions versus single-digit dollars for digital. So WETYR Water works as a premium local-sponsorship, CSR, and awareness vehicle, not a mass ad network. Treat it as the brand and goodwill layer that makes the whole initiative visible and fundable - the municipal water sales remain the plant's economic core.

02

Product two - salt for Morton

The resource is huge and real. Selling it profitably is the interesting part.

The salt is not a rounding error. About 132 tonnes of dissolved salt ride in every million gallons of seawater (less in brackish lagoon water, ~57 to 114 t/MGD). All of it ends up in the concentrate. A plant drawing 10 MGD of seawater is handling on the order of 1,300 tonnes of salt a day - roughly 480,000 tonnes a year. That is a genuine industrial feedstock.

Salt grade~$/tonneWhere it goes
Salt in brine (raw)$11Chlor-alkali feedstock

Vacuum / food grade$260Table salt - Morton's premium line

The precedent, and the local fit

Eilat, Israel already runs the exact model - its seawater RO plant sends brine to evaporation ponds and the Israel Salt Company turns it into table salt. And Port Canaveral is uniquely suited on the logistics side: salt is already one of the port's existing bulk cargoes, so the handling, storage, and offtake ecosystem physically exists on site. Morton sources salt three ways today - solar ponds, rock mines, and vacuum evaporation - so a refined-salt offtake is a plausible partnership.

The honest catch - Florida's climate

The cheap way to make salt is solar evaporation (how Eilat and Morton's Bahamas ponds do it) - but that needs a hot, dry, flat desert. Florida is humid and rainy, so the low-energy solar route is off the table here. That pushes salt recovery toward thermal crystallizers, which are energy-hungry (~20 to 40 kWh per m³ of brine). On bulk NaCl at $11 to $54 a tonne, the crystallizer energy usually eats the margin. The salt wins when you aim at higher-value products - de-icing salt sold north, or magnesium and bromine pulled from the same brine - and count the avoided injection-well cost as part of the return.

03

Product three - pulling nitrogen from the lagoon

The restoration co-benefit, and the cleverest twist: harvest the nitrogen as a crop.

Reverse osmosis rejects and concentrates nitrate, ammonium, and phosphate along with the salt. So if the plant draws nutrient-rich Indian River Lagoon water as part of its feed, it physically removes nitrogen and phosphorus from the estuary - a genuine, fundable restoration action. A 25 MGD plant on lagoon feed captures on the order of 65,000 lbs of nitrogen a year, close to Brevard's entire regulatory reduction target.

The captured nutrients can leave as product two ways:

  • Struvite fertilizer - magnesium-ammonium-phosphate crystals precipitated from the concentrate, a clean slow-release fertilizer already produced commercially at wastewater plants.
  • A biological nitrogen harvest - grow salt-tolerant plants on the nutrient-rich concentrate and sell the biomass, so the nitrogen literally walks out as a crop.

The best nitrogen-pulling plants for a brackish stream

PlantWhy it winsThe product
Seaweed / macroalgae (Ulva, Gracilaria)Highest N uptake of any saltwater plant; grows on excess nitrogen, harvested repeatedlyFertilizer, feed, food
Salicornia / glasswort (sea beans)Halophyte crop irrigated with the brine itself; high N uptake, edibleSea-bean cash crop
Smooth cordgrass (Spartina)High-biomass marsh grass; also builds living shorelinesBiomass / shoreline
Microalgae (photobioreactor)Highest uptake per volume; polishes the concentrated streamBiofuel, feed

The rule that makes it real

Removal only counts if you harvest it. Seaweed and Salicornia win because you physically pull them out and sell them - the nitrogen exits the system for good. Grow a Salicornia field or a seaweed/microalgae biofilter on the RO concentrate, harvest it, and the lagoon's nitrogen becomes a third product alongside the water and the salt. (See the field guide for the full plant playbook.)

Honest note: struvite recovery is proven on wastewater but less established on dilute seawater brine, and the nutrient concentrations are modest - so treat nitrogen capture as a restoration co-benefit and a nutrient-credit generator, not a major revenue line.

04

Toward zero discharge

The vision - and a straight account of what's proven versus aspirational.

Stack the three products and the brine disposal problem shrinks toward nothing: intake water splits into fresh water + salt + a nutrient/crop stream, approaching zero liquid discharge with everything monetized or harvested. That is the elegant end-state. It is also where honesty matters most, because parts of it are proven and parts are still emerging:

ElementStatus
Seawater / brackish RO for drinking waterProven, commercial
Salt from RO brine + salt-company offtake (Eilat)Proven - but with solar climate
Thermal crystallizer salt recovery (no sun needed)Proven but energy-costly
Selling bulk salt at a profit that covers zero-dischargeRarely, without high-value co-products
Struvite N & P fertilizer recoveryProven on wastewater; new on brine
"Everything sold as product" near-zero dischargeEmerging / simulation-stage

Modeled full-recovery seawater plants show salt-and-mineral revenue offsetting 65 to 90% of operating cost - but that is a simulation, not an operating plant. Build the pitch on the proven layers (water + de-icing salt + nutrient credits + avoided disposal) and present full zero-discharge as the roadmap, not today's guarantee.

The pitch, honestly

Build a drinking-water utility Brevard actually needs, sited at Port Canaveral to draw both ocean and lagoon water. Refuse to waste the brine: recover salt for offtake, harvest the captured nitrogen as struvite or a Salicornia crop, and bank the lagoon nutrient removal as restoration credit. The water pays the bills; the salt and nutrients cut the disposal cost and tell a circular-economy story that funders and the county will back. Don't oversell the salt profit - sell the system.
