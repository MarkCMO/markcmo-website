Lagoon cleanup, answered

Straight answers to the most common questions about restoring the Indian River Lagoon - what it costs, how long it takes, whether oysters and seaweed really work, and how a water venture makes the whole thing pay for itself.

01

The plan, in plain terms

Cost, timeline, and the core idea in three answers.

Q1How do you actually clean up the Indian River Lagoon?

Clearing the lagoon comes down to two moves done together: stop new nutrients from washing in, and pull the nutrients already in the water back out. The plan front-loads the fastest, cheapest levers - dredging the soft muck that smothers the bottom, seeding filter feeders like oysters and ribbed mussels on every hard surface, and growing macroalgae that strips nitrogen as it grows. Each is deployed first in small demonstration cells so the water visibly clears before the program scales lagoon-wide.

Q2How much would it cost to restore the lagoon?

There is no single price, which is why the site includes an interactive calculator that builds a program and reads the cost live. A cluster of demonstration cells runs in the low millions. A lagoon-wide program reaches into the hundreds of millions once large-scale muck removal is included, which is why the plan pairs restoration with a self-funding desalination and water venture rather than leaning only on public grants. Muck dredging is the single largest line item.

Q3How long until the water gets clearer?

Inside a small, poorly-flushed demonstration cell, filter feeders and macroalgae can visibly clear the water in a single warm season, because the filters turn the whole volume over faster than the lagoon can refill it. Seaweed grows fastest of all, roughly doubling its mass every few days in Florida summer water. Muck removal and the desalination build are multi-year efforts. The strategy is staged so visible proof arrives early while the slower, heavier work runs in the background.

02

The methods

Oysters, clams, mussels, seaweed, muck - what works and why.

Q4Do oysters really clean the water, and how many are needed?

Yes. A single adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons a day, pulling algae and suspended sediment out of the water column. The catch is scale: clearing open lagoon water requires oysters in the tens of millions, which is why the plan concentrates them in confined cells first and pairs them with other filter feeders. The oyster economics page works through hatchery seed cost, mats, and cages per acre so the numbers are transparent rather than hand-waved.

Q5Oysters vs clams vs mussels - which is best?

All three filter water, but they fit different jobs. Oysters build reef and mount easily on docks and mats. Hard clams filter well but sit buried in sediment and are slower to deploy at scale. The ribbed mussel is the quiet standout for this system: it self-recruits, tolerates low salinity and warm water, packs densely along marsh edges, and asks for almost no hatchery cost. The plan leans on a mix, weighted toward mussels and oysters for cost and self-reproduction.

Q6What is a demonstration cell and why start there?

A demonstration cell is a small, stagnant, nutrient-choked stretch of water - typically a dead-end residential canal or a tributary mouth - treated as a self-contained proof of concept. Because the volume is small and the flushing is poor, filters and macroalgae can turn the water visibly clear within a season. Cells are chosen as the worst offenders on purpose: that is where cleanup shows fastest, builds public support, and de-risks the case for scaling lagoon-wide. See the demonstration cells page for the candidate canals.

Q7What is muck and why does it have to be removed?

Muck is the black, soupy organic sediment that has accumulated on the lagoon bottom over decades of runoff. It smothers seagrass, blocks light, and continuously releases stored nitrogen and phosphorus back into the water even after external sources are cut. Removing it is the single most expensive line item in any serious plan, but it is unavoidable: leave the muck in place and it keeps re-fueling algae blooms no matter how many filter feeders are added on top.

Q8Can seaweed like Ulva clean the lagoon, and how fast does it grow?

Macroalgae like Ulva - sea lettuce - strip dissolved nitrogen directly out of the water as they grow, then get harvested and hauled out, permanently removing those nutrients. In warm Florida water Ulva roughly doubles its mass every three to four days, so a single pound scales into a working biofilter within a couple of months. The aquaculture pages walk through the grow-out math, how much to start with, and which macroalgae tolerate 90-degree summer water.

03

The venture and the money

How the cleanup funds itself instead of waiting on grants.

Q9Does the desalination plant help the lagoon or just make water?

It does more than make water. The plant is sited to draw from and discharge around the lagoon system in a way that pulls nitrogen out as a designed function, not a side effect. It also removes salt for sale and produces clean drinking water, giving the whole restoration a revenue engine instead of a permanent grant dependency. The idea is to make lagoon cleanup pay for itself by attaching it to products people and industry already buy. The desalination plan lays it out.

Q10What is WETYR Water?

WETYR Water is the bottled-water arm of the venture: clean drinking water produced by the desalination plant and given away free, funded by businesses advertising on the label. It turns the plant's output into a public-facing product that markets the restoration, builds goodwill across Brevard, and creates an advertising revenue stream - all while the underlying process pulls nitrogen out of the lagoon. Free water people actually want becomes the front door to a self-funding cleanup.

Q11Where does the nitrogen and phosphorus come from?

The nitrogen and phosphorus feeding the blooms come from three main sources: fertilizer and stormwater runoff off lawns, farms, and pavement; wastewater, including treatment-plant discharge and aging septic systems; and the muck on the bottom, which keeps releasing what decades of the first two deposited. The feasibility brief quantifies each input across Brevard's coastline so the plan targets the biggest, cheapest-to-cut sources first rather than spreading effort evenly.

04

Context and getting involved

How this fits the existing effort, and how to help.

Q12Is this the same as the Save Our Indian River Lagoon plan?

No. This is an independent, privately-authored analysis prepared by Mark Gabrielli that draws on the same public science - FDEP data, the county Save Our Indian River Lagoon plan, and UF and Florida Tech research - but is not affiliated with or endorsed by the county program. It is offered as planning-grade material for public discussion, with an emphasis on self-funding mechanisms and fast, visible demonstration results that complement the existing public effort.

Q13Why hasn't this been done already?

Pieces of it have - the county has dredged muck and funded projects for years. What is usually missing is a self-funding engine and a staged, prove-it-first structure. Most plans depend entirely on public grants, so they move at the speed of budgets. Pairing restoration with a desalination and water business changes the economics, and starting in small demonstration cells produces visible wins that build the political will to scale. The barrier has been financing and sequencing, not biology.

Q14How can Brevard County or a private funder get involved?

The fastest path is to fund a first cluster of demonstration cells, which is small enough to launch quickly and visible enough to build support for everything after. The funding page lays out the launch sequence and the venture page frames the investment case for the self-funding side. Anyone - county staff, a private funder, or a partner interested in the water and desalination business - can reach the initiative directly at mark@markcmo.com to discuss a first cell.
