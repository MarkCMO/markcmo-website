01

Reverse-engineering the Ulva math

Start from the nitrogen we need gone and work back to acres, biomass, and time.

Ulva (sea lettuce) is 2 to 5% nitrogen by dry weight - it luxury-uptakes nitrogen when the water is rich, which the lagoon is. Field studies in nutrient-loaded water measure removal at about 0.88 grams of nitrogen per square meter per day. Working the chain backward:

N per lb Ulva: at 3.5% N (dry) → 1 lb N needs ~29 lb dry = ~230 lb wet Ulva
N per acre: 0.88 g N/m²/day × 4,047 m² × 365 = ~2,900 lb N/acre/yr (year-round)
De-rated for summer die-back (~7 months) → ~1,660 lb N/acre/yr

Why Ulva beats bivalves on nitrogen per acre

Ulva takes up dissolved nitrogen directly and grows explosively (it can double in ~3.5 days in the IRL), so an acre of cages removes roughly 3 to 6 times the nitrogen of an acre of oyster reef - and produces ~100 tonnes of wet biomass a year to sell. The catch: it only counts if you harvest it, and it needs continuous cropping.

The summer-heat catch

Common sea lettuce (Ulva lactuca) stalls above ~28 to 30°C, so the IRL's hot summer causes die-back - de-rate annual removal to roughly 7 productive months, or grow a heat-tolerant species like Ulva ohnoi to run closer to year-round. Lab-optimal numbers (25 to 34 g dry/m²/day) need controlled tanks - plan around the field-realistic 5 to 15 range.

Which species for 90°F water, and how to start

For the lagoon's bath-warm summer (~90°F / 32°C), switch species. Gracilaria (a red alga - the Florida-proven pick: warm- and salinity-tolerant, with agar value) and Ulva ohnoi (the heat-tolerant sea lettuce) both thrive where common U. lactuca dies back; Chaetomorpha is a bombproof warm-water scrubber. Run a warm species through summer and U. lactuca in the cooler months for year-round removal.

Seeding is cheap, because it self-multiplies. Ulva doubles roughly every 3.5 days, so a nursery started with just ~50 to 200 lb fills a full demonstration acre in ~6 weeks and reaches TMDL scale (~40 acres) within a single warm season. The limit is cage build-out and harvest labor, not the seaweed.

02

The filtration lineup

Who actually pulls the most nitrogen - macroalgae, oysters, clams, mussels, snails, sponges - ranked honestly.

Two different jobs, and the winners do them together. Macroalgae strips dissolved nitrogen before it becomes algae; bivalves filter the particulate load (phytoplankton, detritus) after. Run together, that is IMTA - the strongest design.

The one-line answer on organisms

For raw nitrogen removed per acre, harvested macroalgae wins. For a self-sustaining, low-labor cleaner in brackish IRL water, the ribbed mussel is the most underrated pick. Oysters and clams add habitat, shoreline, and cheap scale. Snails, sponges, and tunicates are not the tool for nitrogen removal. The best system runs macroalgae + mussels/oysters together.

03

The shrimp-funded loop (IMTA)

Shrimp add nitrogen - so make them pay the extractive species that take it back out.

Shrimp are a fed species: feed them, they grow, they excrete ammonia - on their own a nitrogen source, the opposite of what the lagoon needs. Open-lagoon shrimp farming would make things worse. The fix is Integrated Multi-Trophic Aquaculture: a contained loop where the fed species funds the cleanup.

Shrimp - fed

The cash crop. Fresh, local, never-frozen Florida shrimp sells at a premium against a ~90% imported/frozen market. Produces the waste the others eat.

Seaweed - extractive

Ulva takes up the dissolved nitrogen directly, grows fast, and is harvested for revenue - the primary nitrogen sink.

Shellfish - extractive

Mussels, oysters, and clams filter the particulate waste and phytoplankton, polishing the water before any discharge.

The balance that makes it work

Size the seaweed and shellfish to recapture the shrimp's nitrogen - studies show extractive species can pull back 30 to 100% of the fed nitrogen. Do it right and the whole loop is net-neutral or net-negative on nitrogen while producing three cash crops instead of one. The shrimp revenue funds the extractive side that does the cleanup and earns the restoration credits.

The non-negotiable rule

Contained, and polished before any discharge. Shrimp live in a lined pond, biofloc, or recirculating system - never the open lagoon. The seaweed and shellfish scrub the effluent; only clean water goes back. Pair it with the desalination plant for salinity control and you have a closed shrimp + seaweed + shellfish + water campus at Port Canaveral.

The net-nitrogen balance

Feed N in: 30 t feed × 5.6% N = ~1,700 kg N/yr (~3,750 lb)
Out via shrimp harvest (~25% retained): ~420 kg N
Out via Ulva + shellfish harvest (sized to capture the rest): ~1,280 kg N
→ Discharge to lagoon ≈ 0 (net-neutral); draw lagoon feedwater and oversize the extractive side → net-negative
What the module teaches

Shrimp is ~75% of the revenue - it carries the whole operation, and the seaweed and shellfish that do the cleanup ride on it. Balanced right, the module discharges essentially no nitrogen; fed with lagoon water and tuned extractive-heavy, it removes net nitrogen from the lagoon while turning a profit. That is the model: a food-and-water business whose product is a cleaner lagoon.

Planning-grade illustration. Intensive shrimp (biofloc/RAS) is capital- and operations-demanding, feed and energy are the main risks, and Florida has no nitrogen-credit market yet - so treat credits as upside and validate stocking ratios, prices, and the nitrogen balance with an aquaculture engineer before committing capital.

Three myths to keep out of the pitch

The three sexiest seaweed stories do not apply to Ulva: the hydrocolloids (agar, carrageenan, alginate) come from red and brown seaweed; the methane-reducing cattle feed is Asparagopsis (a red alga), not Ulva; and Ulva biofuel loses money. Ulva's honest lanes are food, biostimulant, feed, and ulvan - plus paid ecosystem services.

The economics, plainly

Land-based tanks yield ~25-40 dry tonnes/ha/yr at a production cost of ~$200-300/dry tonne. Peer-reviewed work is blunt: seaweed only pencils on high-value food/products plus paid ecosystem services, not on biomass tonnage. Florida's warm water rules out kelp and nori - Gracilaria and Ulva are the subtropical candidates (UF/IFAS is running trials on a NOAA grant).

The nitrogen-credit upside

Elsewhere, growers get paid for the nitrogen they remove: Long Island Sound valued seaweed bioextraction at ~$1,200/acre, and oyster nitrogen credits trade at $8.56 to $32.69/lb (Maryland has hit thousands). Florida has no such market yet - so bank credits as upside, and stand one up (a Water Quality Enhancement Area) as part of the play. See funding.

The honest business case

Don't sell seaweed tonnage or Ulva biofuel. Sell the system: shrimp is the cash, seaweed and shellfish are the cleanup plus secondary revenue plus nitrogen credits, and the ribbed mussel is the underrated engine of cheap, self-sustaining nitrogen removal. The venture is a food-and-water business whose product is a cleaner lagoon.
