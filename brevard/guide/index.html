The field guide

Every tool in the plan, in plain English - what it is, how it works, what it costs, how fast it works, and where it fits. No jargon, no hand-waving.

00

The big idea: a treatment train

No single fix cleans a lagoon. They work as a chain, each handling a different stage of the water's journey.

Think of the water moving from the land, across the shoreline, and out into the open lagoon. Each fix intercepts it at a different point:

01

Baffle boxes

The cheapest pound of nitrogen in the whole plan, installed in weeks.

A baffle box is a large concrete vault buried under a storm drain. Runoff flows through it on the way to the lagoon, and internal walls ("baffles") slow the water so sediment, trash, and nutrients drop out and get trapped instead of washing into the water. They come in two generations:

TypeWhat it removesHow
First-generation sediment boxSand, trash, coarse sediment and the nutrients bound to themSettling chambers + screens; caught material is vacuumed out periodically
Nutrient-Separating Baffle Box (NSBB)The above plus dissolved nitrogen and phosphorusAdds a bed of sorption media (Bold & Gold / BAM - a sand, clay and recycled-material blend) that chemically grabs dissolved nutrients as water passes through

Why it wins

At about $88 per pound of nitrogen per year, stormwater treatment is the cheapest capital category in Brevard's own lagoon plan. A single unit is a precast structure dropped in over one construction season, and it works the day it is connected. The Bold & Gold media lasts ~20 years and can be rinsed and reused.

The catch

They only treat what flows through them, so they must be placed at the highest-load outfalls to matter. And they need maintenance - the trapped sediment must be vacuumed out on a schedule, or a full box stops working and can even release its captured load.

Bottom line: the first thing to buy. Cheapest dollars per pound, fastest to install, lowest science risk.

02

Demonstration cells

The fastest way to show visibly clear water - engineered, in a single season.

A demonstration cell is a small, contained, badly-flushed water body - a dead-end residential canal, a marina basin, a cove - that we treat as an all-at-once proof of concept. These spots are the worst offenders (small volume, terrible flushing, high nutrient), which is exactly why they respond fastest: you can filter the water faster than fresh water replaces it, so it visibly clears in one season. In the open, well-flushed lagoon the same effort would be invisible.

Each cell gets the full stack at once:

  • Vacuum the muck first so the biggest nutrient source stops feeding blooms.
  • Broadcast clam seed across the cleared floor - buried filter feeders, no structure needed.
  • Hang oyster mats and cages on every dock piling - biomass filtering the day it goes in.
  • Float treatment-wetland rafts at the dead end where the stormwater comes in.
  • Add aeration to hold oxygen up and keep the water circulating past the filters.
The design rule

Size the filter-feeder population so total daily clearance beats the cell's volume divided by its flushing time. A typical 1,500 ft canal holds ~5.4M gallons and flushes in ~14 days, so it needs ~385,000 gallons cleared per day. One drone drop of clam seed across the floor, plus oysters on the docks, clears several times that - and the water gets measurably clearer within 12 to 18 months. Cost runs roughly $80k to $280k per cell, most of it the muck vacuum.

03

Clams vs oysters

Both are living filters. They cost, deploy, and pay off very differently.

Both are bivalves that filter algae and nutrients out of the water. The difference is how they live: oysters build hard reefs above the bottom, clams bury themselves in the sediment. That one fact drives everything else.

Hard clam (Mercenaria)Eastern oyster (Crassostrea)
How it livesBuried in the sedimentCemented onto a hard reef

Extra benefitsClears water for seagrassHabitat, shoreline wave-break, fisheries
Best roleCheap biomass at scalePermanent living infrastructure

Use clams when

You want the most filtering capacity per dollar, fast to deploy, over large soft-bottom areas. Clams are the scale play - dirt cheap to seed, no construction - but they take a year-plus to mature and seed survival is low, so you oversize the drop.

Use oysters when

You want permanent, self-sustaining reef that also protects shoreline, builds habitat, and can filter immediately when placed as cages or mats. Oysters cost far more per acre (the money is in the reef structure and labor, not the animals) but they are living infrastructure that reproduces and lasts.

In practice you use both: clams broadcast across the soft bottom for cheap volume, oysters built into reefs and living shorelines at the edges. See the calculator to weigh them, or the feasibility brief for the full oyster-reef sizing and the exact hatchery costs.

04

The plants

Three plant families for three zones. Get the plant matched to the place.

Seagrass - the open lagoon bottom

Seagrass is the goal, not just a tool: it is the nursery of the whole lagoon and what the manatees eat. The 2011 algae superbloom blocked the sunlight and killed roughly 47,000 acres (~60%) of it, which is what triggered the manatee starvation crisis. It comes back only when the water is clear enough for light to reach the bottom - which is the whole point of the filters and muck removal. The key species:

SpeciesRoleWhere
Shoal grass (Halodule wrightii)Pioneer - the first to establish, used for replantingShallow, variable light
Manatee grass (Syringodium filiforme)Mixed bedsMid-depth (~5 ft)
Turtle grass (Thalassia testudinum)Needs the most light - a sign of healthy waterClearer southern lagoon
Widgeon grass (Ruppia maritima)Tolerates low, variable salinityVery shallow (<1 ft)

It is replanted from nursery-grown shoal-grass fragments set in peat pots and protected by mesh cages that keep manatees and fish from grazing the young plants. Brevard Zoo's Restore Our Shores and Sea & Shoreline's "GrowSAV" both run IRL seagrass nurseries; clams and oysters are deliberately paired with seagrass because filtering the water is what lets the light back in.

Marsh grass and mangroves - the shoreline

At the water's edge, smooth cordgrass (Spartina), black needlerush (Juncus), and the three mangroves (red, black, white) hold the shoreline together with their roots, break incoming waves, and soak up nutrients from runoff before it reaches open water. Combined with an oyster reef at the toe, this is a "living shoreline" - it does the job of a seawall while also filtering and building habitat.

Floating treatment wetlands - the ponds and canals

These are planted rafts floated on stormwater ponds and canals. The plant roots hang down into the water, hosting the microbes that strip nitrogen and growing tissue that you harvest to physically export the nutrients. A full-scale study at Wickham Park in Melbourne (local data) measured the best performers:

SpeciesNitrogen fixed (g/m²/yr)
Soft rush (Juncus effusus)13.5
Bentgrass (Agrostis alba)13.2
Duck potato (Sagittaria lancifolia)12.1
Pickerelweed (Pontederia cordata)lower N, good P uptake

Zonation in one line: floating wetlands strip nutrients upstream, marsh and mangroves buffer the edge, and clearer water lets seagrass recover on the bottom.

05

Muck removal

The single biggest nitrogen source - the "black mayonnaise" on the bottom - and how it is actually dug out.

Decades of runoff and dead algae have settled into a soupy black organic ooze on the lagoon floor. This "muck" is the single largest nutrient source - about 35% of the nitrogen - because it constantly leaks stored nitrogen and phosphorus back into the water. Removing it is the biggest permanent win, and also the slowest and most capital-heavy. Here is how it is done:

Step 1 - Dredge it (hydraulically, not mechanically)

Because muck is fine and soupy, the lagoon projects use hydraulic dredging: a rotating auger head with a suction pump lifts the muck as a slurry inside an enclosed pipeline. That keeps the fine, contaminated material from re-clouding the water - the decisive advantage over a mechanical clamshell bucket, which smears and resuspends muck. Florida Tech even built a custom fine-sediment suction head for the work.

Step 2 - Dewater it in geotubes

The slurry is pumped into giant woven fabric bags called geotextile tubes ("geotubes") at a management area onshore. The fabric holds the solids while water seeps out over ~5-7 days. Critically, that drained water is nutrient-rich, so it is not returned untreated - it runs through a temporary treatment system to strip the nitrogen and phosphorus first, or the whole point is defeated.

Step 3 - Reuse the dried muck

Once dry, the geotubes are opened and the muck is trucked out and spread on farmland west of I-95 as a soil amendment (after testing for contaminants). Emerging "muck-to-product" pathways from Florida Tech turn waste biomass into biochar (which is being tested to fight algae blooms and boost seagrass) and blend treated sediment into concrete aggregate.

The cheaper in-place alternatives (and their trade-offs)

Where full removal is too costly, three in-place options manage the muck instead of removing it: sand capping (bury it under clean sand - cheap, but a storm can breach it), alum treatment (aluminum locks up phosphorus - effective but can starve the bottom water of oxygen), and aeration (bubbling oxygen into the sediment - an IRL canal pilot raised oxygen and cut ammonium, but it is an ongoing energy cost, not a permanent fix). Removal deletes the source; the in-place options only hold it down.

06

The rest of the toolkit

The source-control and water-supply levers that round out the plan.

Septic-to-sewer + plant upgrades

Sewage and septic are ~26% of the nitrogen. Connecting septic homes to sewer works but is slow and costly ($20k+/home). Far more cost-effective: upgrading treatment plants to strip nutrients - the Palm Bay-type upgrade removes ~11,000 lbs of nitrogen a year at just $125/lb, the best big-block buy in the plan.

Fertilizer control

The cheapest lever of all, and the most overrated. Lawn fertilizer is only ~6% of the load, and Brevard's ordinance already cut ~46,000 lbs a year. Worth keeping, but it is a supporting policy, not a solution - sewage and muck are where the nitrogen actually is.

Desalination

A drinking-water utility that skims some nutrients as a bonus - not a lagoon cure. It cannot pump a 208-billion-gallon lagoon clean, and its membranes concentrate nutrients into a brine that must be injected deep underground, never returned to the lagoon. Build it for water supply; see the financial model.

Nutrient credits

Florida lets you sell certified nitrogen reductions as credits to polluters who owe cleanup. Benchmark prices run $5-50/lb (oyster credits have traded near $400/lb elsewhere). Real upside that helps repay reef and clam work - but Florida's market is young, so treat it as a bonus, not the bankable core.

How it all fits

Rank everything by cost per pound of nitrogen and the order is clear: baffle boxes and demonstration cells first (cheap, fast, visible), cheap plant upgrades and clam seeding next (big blocks at low cost), and muck dredging, oyster reef, and desalination as the larger permanent builds once the momentum and funding are there. Every fix removes a different pound in a different place - together they clear the water; alone, none of them do.
