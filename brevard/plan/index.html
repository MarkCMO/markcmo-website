Brevard Lagoon Initiative / Action Plan
The fast, cheap, visible path

The action plan

Optimizing for the most nitrogen removed per dollar in the least time. The surprising result: fast-growing macroalgae and self-reproducing shellfish are the cheapest nitrogen in the lagoon, while the oyster mega-reef and the desalination plant are the slow, expensive scale plays - not the quick wins.

What the ranking tells you

Macroalgae (Ulva, ~$36/lb) and self-reproducing bivalves - clams (~$50) and ribbed mussels (~$160) - are now the cheapest nitrogen in the lagoon, and they regrow themselves instead of needing to be rebuilt. Baffle boxes ($88) stay the best gray-infrastructure buy, installable in weeks, and cheap plant upgrades ($125/lb, 11,000+ lbs each) are the best big-block buys. Oyster reef ($392) and septic-to-sewer ($443 to $872) run 11 to 24x the cost of Ulva per pound - worth doing at scale later, wrong place to start.

Two footnotes. Muck dredging looks mid-pack per annual pound but removed legacy nitrogen at only ~$10/lb at Eau Gallie and deletes 35% of the load permanently. And the top of this list understates its own case: Ulva, clams, and mussels reproduce themselves, so the seed is a one-time cost while the filtration compounds - and the biomass can be harvested and sold as seaweed or an IMTA shrimp crop, which no pipe or drainfield can do. Desalination is left off this chart on purpose - it is funded by water and salt revenue, not by cost per pound.

02

The three-layer strategy

Stop new nitrogen cheaply, prove a visible win fast, then buy the biggest cheap blocks.
Layer 1 · 0-6 mo

Turn off the cheapest taps

Baffle boxes at the worst outfalls plus fertilizer enforcement. Cheapest dollars per pound, installed in weeks, no science risk.

Layer 2 · 6-18 mo

Win visibly in the canals

Five demonstration cells: vacuum muck, grow Ulva biofilters, seed clams and ribbed mussels, hang oysters, float wetlands, aerate. Water clears in one season - the proof that unlocks funding.

Layer 3 · 1-3 yr

Buy the biggest cheap blocks

Cheap plant upgrades (~11,000 lbs each at $125/lb), muck removal in the worst hotspots, and scaled macroalgae and shellfish beds. Large permanent removals at low unit cost - some of it harvestable for revenue.

Why not lead with oysters or desal

A well-flushed open lagoon will not show a fast visible clarity change from bivalves at any achievable density - that is a measured finding. So the oyster mega-reef pays off slowly and at scale, and desalination is a 5-to-7-year build. The fast levers are different: baffle boxes, muck vacuuming, and - inside the small, poorly-flushed demonstration cells - fast-growing macroalgae and dense shellfish, which turn a confined canal visibly clear in a single warm season because the filters lap the water faster than it refills. Lead with those and you get measurable nitrogen cuts and clear water inside 18 months, for single-digit millions.

04

Financial models

Nutrient-credit revenue, and the desalination utility pro-forma.

What the state actually pays per pound of nitrogen

Short answer: there is no single Florida "credit price," and the state does not run a per-pound checkbook you invoice. Three real mechanisms pay for removed nitrogen, and they pay very differently - so the honest planning number depends on which one you use.

  • SOIRL project cost-share - the real money today. Brevard's Save Our Indian River Lagoon half-cent sales tax (up to ~$586M over 10 years) funds restoration projects by multiplying the nitrogen a project removes by an allowable cost per pound. The published anchor: the Quick Connects program pays $1,600 per pound of nitrogen kept out of the lagoon (septic side). You get paid by having the project adopted into the county plan and funded - not by selling a commodity credit.
  • Water Quality Enhancement Area (WQEA) credits - the future line. Florida's 2023 WQEA law (s. 403.0752, F.S.) lets a private entity build treatment, certify the nitrogen it removes as credits, and sell them to permittees who owe an offset. The rule was still being finalized through 2024 to 2026 (SB 848), credits currently sell to governmental entities, and no standard Florida transaction price is published yet - real, but not bankable this year.
  • Benchmark from mature markets. Where nutrient trading is established (Chesapeake Bay), a documented oyster-nitrogen deal paid ~$46/lb; Maryland credits have ranged from $75/lb into the low thousands; a risk-adjusted high estimate is ~$190/lb per year.

Modeled as a tradeable-credit line on an 850-acre reef removing 380,239 lbs N/yr, even conservative prices repay the build:

Aquaculture revenue (macroalgae, shellfish & IMTA shrimp)

The cheapest cleanup levers are also the only ones that pay you back. The same Ulva, clams, and mussels doing the filtering are a harvestable crop, and stacking shrimp on top in an integrated multi-trophic loop (IMTA) turns the filtration beds into a working farm. It is the one line on this page that generates cash from day one instead of drawing on grants - the full unit economics, grow-out math, and revenue stack live on the aquaculture and venture pages.

Three revenue engines, one cleanup

Nutrient credits monetize the pounds removed, the desalination utility sells water and salt, and the aquaculture beds sell biomass and shrimp. Restoration stops being a pure cost center and starts carrying part of its own weight.

The standard structure: a public or nonprofit partner (Brevard County, a city, a university, or Brevard Zoo's Restore Our Shores) is the grantee of record; the private venture is the contractor, technology provider, or operator, and owns the pieces it can hold outright - the nutrient-credit area and the desalination utility. Note: the RESTORE Act does not apply - that is Gulf-coast-only money; Brevard is Atlantic.

The honest verdict

Oysters are the best living answer at scale, and desalination is a real self-funding water utility - but neither works on dirty, stagnant water, and neither is the fast lever. Muck removal and sewage control come first (61% of the nitrogen); baffle boxes, muck vacuuming, and fast-growing macroalgae plus dense shellfish in the demonstration cells deliver the visible win in one season; and the big permanent builds - the oyster mega-reef, the desalination utility - follow the proof. The aquaculture beds are the cheapest nitrogen of all and pay part of their own way. Fund the flagship, and the rest follows.
